# Chapter 51: The Necklace

## Theme & Summary

**Theme:** The story is a powerful critique of vanity, materialism, and the destructive consequences of pride. It presents a classic tragic irony — a woman's desire to appear richer than she is leads to a life of real poverty, all for a necklace that turns out to be worthless. The story explores themes of social aspiration, the trap of appearances, and the crushing weight of unnecessary sacrifice.

**Summary:** Mathilde Loisel is a pretty, charming woman born into a family of clerks, who constantly laments her middle-class life and dreams of luxury. Her husband, a clerk, secures an invitation to a grand ball for her. Mathilde borrows a diamond necklace from her wealthy friend, Mme Forestier, to wear at the ball. At the ball, she is the center of attention and has the time of her life. Afterward, she discovers the necklace is missing. Unable to find it, she and her husband replace it with an identical-looking one that costs 36,000 francs — taking on massive debt. The next ten years are spent in grinding poverty, hard labor, and sacrifice to repay the debt. Mathilde ages beyond recognition. One day, she encounters Mme Forestier in the street and finally confesses the truth — only to learn from Mme Forestier that the original necklace was fake, worth at most 500 francs.

## Character List

- **Mathilde Loisel:** The protagonist; a beautiful but discontented, vain woman whose desire for luxury destroys her life.
- **Monsieur Loisel:** Mathilde's kind, sacrificing, and devoted husband who works tirelessly to repay the debt.
- **Mme Jeanne Forestier:** Mathilde's wealthy friend; generous in lending the necklace; reveals the ironic truth at the end.

## Key Quotes

1. "She was one of those pretty and charming girls who are sometimes, as if by a mistake of destiny, born into a family of clerks."
2. "She had no dresses, no jewels, nothing. And she loved nothing but that."
3. "She danced with intoxication, with passion, made drunk by pleasure."
4. "It was all over with her friend. Madame Forestier, deeply moved, took both her hands. 'Oh, my poor Mathilde! But mine was imitation. It was worth at the very most five hundred francs!'"
