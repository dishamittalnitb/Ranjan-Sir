# Chapter 21: In the Kingdom of Fools

## Theme & Summary

**Theme:** The story is a satirical folktale that mocks foolish rulers and unjust governance. It conveys that wisdom and wit can triumph over absurdity and tyranny, and that foolishness eventually leads to its own undoing.

**Summary:** In the Kingdom of Fools, both the king and the minister are idiots who impose bizarre rules — everyone must work at night and sleep during the day, and everything costs the same (one duddu). A guru and his disciple arrive and are initially amused, but the guru, foreseeing danger, leaves. The disciple stays behind, lured by cheap food. One day, a wall collapses on a thief; a chain of absurd logic in the king's court leads to blaming the merchant (whose wall it was), then the bricklayer (who built it badly), then a dancing girl, then a goldsmith — ultimately, the disciple is sentenced to death because the stake cannot penetrate the goldsmith and the disciple's body fits. The guru returns and uses his wit to save the disciple: he tricks the king and minister into believing that the first to be executed on the new stake will become the next king. The power-hungry king and minister execute each other secretly, dying. The people, relieved, make the guru and disciple the new rulers.

## Character List

- **The King:** Foolish ruler who decrees absurd laws.
- **The Minister:** Equally foolish; the king's accomplice in stupidity.
- **The Guru:** Wise saint who foresees danger and later rescues his disciple through cleverness.
- **The Disciple:** Naive young man who stays in the kingdom for cheap food and nearly gets executed.
- **The Goldsmith:** A weak, dishonest man who cannot be impaled properly.
- **The Merchant/Bricklayer/Dancing Girl:** Characters caught in the absurd chain of blame.

## Key Quotes

1. "In the kingdom of fools, both the king and the minister were idiots."
2. "They decided that night was day and day was night."
3. "Everything costs the same — one duddu."
4. "Whoever dies first on this stake will become the king in his next life."
5. "In the land of fools, it is better to keep one's wits about one."
