# Chapter 38: How to Tell Wild Animals

## Theme & Summary

**Theme:** The poem is a humorous, tongue-in-cheek guide to identifying wild animals — not by their scientific characteristics, but by the (often fatal) way they interact with humans. Through its witty, absurd suggestions, the poem satirizes the human tendency to feel superior to wild creatures while also highlighting the danger and majesty of these animals.

**Summary:** The poem offers a series of comically impractical methods for identifying wild animals. If you go to the jungle and a large tawny beast roars at you, it's an Asian Lion. If a noble beast with black stripes on a yellow ground greets you, it's a Bengal Tiger — but if it eats you, it might really be a tiger. The leopard is identified by its repeated "lep and lep" attack; the bear by its tight hug. The crocodile is identified by its weeping (crocodile tears), and the chameleon by the fact that you simply cannot see it because it blends in. The humor lies in the contradiction — you can only identify these animals by being their victim.

## Literary Devices

- **Rhyme Scheme:** ABAB (alternating rhyme in quatrains).
- **Humor and Irony:** The entire poem is built on situational irony — the "methods" of identification involve being attacked.
- **Alliteration:** "lep and lep again," "whom to see," "yard you find"
- **Personification:** Animals like the tiger and hyena are attributed human social behaviors ("he'll give you just one more caress").
- **Wordplay / Pun:** "Lep and lep" = sounds like leopard's movement but also "leap."
- **Tone:** Humorous, playful, satirical.

## Key Quotes

1. "If ever you should go by chance / To jungles in the east"
2. "The Bengal Tiger to discern. A noble beast with black stripes on a yellow ground."
3. "A true Chameleon is small, / A lizard sort of thing; / He hasn't any ears at all, / And not a single wing."
4. "If there's nothing on the tree, / 'Tis the chameleon you see."
